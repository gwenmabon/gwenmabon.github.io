A DSP's profitability hinges on predicting user engagement accurately.
This article covers click-through rate (CTR) and conversion rate (CVR)
prediction as probability estimation problems. We decompose the Brier
Score, explain why calibration matters more than discrimination in adtech,
introduce proper scoring rules, and tackle the selection bias inherent in
training on data generated by a previous bidding policy using Inverse
Propensity Weighting (IPW).

## Why Calibration Matters More Than AUC

In most ML applications, we care about *ranking* (AUC). In adtech,
we care about *calibrated probabilities* because the predicted
probability feeds directly into the bid:

$$
\text{bid} = \hat{p}(\text{conversion} \mid x) \times \text{payout} \times \text{shading\_factor}
$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> If \(\hat{p}\) is systematically overestimated by 20%, we overbid by
> 20% on every impression. Good AUC with bad calibration means
> **correct ranking but wrong prices**. We win the right
> impressions but pay too much.

### A Concrete Example

Consider two models scoring 10,000 impressions:

| Model | AUC | Calibration Error | Profit |
|-------|:---:|:-----------------:|-------:|
| Model A (well-calibrated) | 0.78 | 0.02 | +$12,400 |
| Model B (miscalibrated) | 0.82 | 0.15 | -$3,200 |

Model B has better discrimination but loses money because its probability
estimates are off.

## The Brier Score and Its Decomposition

### Definition

The Brier Score is the mean squared error of probabilistic predictions:

$$
\text{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2
$$

where \(\hat{p}_i \in [0,1]\) is the predicted probability and
\(y_i \in \{0,1\}\) is the outcome. Lower is better.

### The Murphy Decomposition

The Brier Score decomposes into three interpretable terms:

$$
\text{BS} = \underbrace{\text{REL}}_{\text{Calibration}} -
            \underbrace{\text{RES}}_{\text{Resolution}} +
            \underbrace{\text{UNC}}_{\text{Uncertainty}}
$$

- **Reliability (REL)**: measures calibration error.
  \(\text{REL} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k(\bar{p}_k - \bar{y}_k)^2\).
  Lower is better.
- **Resolution (RES)**: measures how well the model separates
  groups with different base rates. Higher is better.
- **Uncertainty (UNC)**: \(\bar{y}(1-\bar{y})\), the irreducible
  variance of the outcome. Fixed for a given dataset.

> [!NOTE]
> The decomposition reveals that a good Brier Score requires
> *both* calibration (low REL) *and* discrimination (high
> RES). In adtech, we optimize REL first because it directly affects
> bid accuracy.

## Log-Loss: The Proper Scoring Rule

While the Brier Score is intuitive, the industry standard loss function
for CTR models is the **log-loss** (cross-entropy):

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[
  y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)
\bigr]
$$

### Why Log-Loss?

1. It is a **strictly proper scoring rule**: it is uniquely
   minimized when \(\hat{p}_i = p_i\) (the true probability).
2. It penalizes confident wrong predictions *exponentially*
   harder than the Brier Score.
3. It is the natural loss for logistic regression and neural
   networks with sigmoid output.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A "proper" scoring rule guarantees that the model has no incentive
> to report anything other than its true belief. This is critical
> in adtech: we need the model to be *honest* about its
> uncertainty, because that uncertainty propagates to bid prices.

### Relationship to KL Divergence

Log-loss can be written as:

$$
\mathcal{L} = H(Y) + D_{\text{KL}}(p \,\|\, \hat{p})
$$

where \(H(Y)\) is the entropy of the true distribution and
\(D_{\text{KL}}\) is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. Minimizing
log-loss is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence from
the true distribution.

## Calibration Techniques

### Platt Scaling

Fit a logistic regression on the model's raw scores:

$$
\hat{p}_{\text{calibrated}} = \sigma(a \cdot s + b)
$$

where \(s\) is the uncalibrated score and \(a, b\) are learned on a
held-out calibration set.

### Isotonic Regression

A non-parametric approach: fit a monotonically non-decreasing step
function mapping raw scores to calibrated probabilities. More
flexible than Platt scaling but requires more data and can overfit
on small calibration sets.

### Temperature Scaling

For neural networks: divide logits by a learned temperature \(T > 0\):

$$
\hat{p}_{\text{calibrated}} = \sigma\!\left(\frac{\text{logit}}{T}\right)
$$

\(T > 1\) softens overconfident predictions; \(T < 1\) sharpens underconfident ones.

## Selection Bias and Inverse Propensity Weighting

### The Problem

Training data in adtech is *not* i.i.d. It is generated by a
previous bidding policy. We only observe outcomes for impressions we
*won* (and at the price we *bid*).

> [!WARNING]
> This is "bandit feedback": we observe the reward for the action we
> took, not the counterfactual. Naively training on this data introduces
> **selection bias**. The model learns to predict well only on
> the slice of traffic it already bids on.

### IPW Correction

Inverse Propensity Weighting reweights each training example by the
inverse of its probability of being in the training set:

$$
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{IPW}} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \frac{1}{e(x_i)}\bigl[
    y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)
  \bigr]
$$

where \(e(x_i) = P(\text{win} \mid x_i)\) is the **propensity score**: the probability that impression \(x_i\) was won under the logging
policy.

- Impressions we rarely win get upweighted (they are underrepresented).
- Impressions we always win get downweighted (they dominate the sample).

### Practical Considerations

1. **Propensity estimation**: \(e(x)\) is estimated from the historical
   win-rate model — itself a learned quantity, so errors compound.
2. **Clipping**: extreme weights (\(1/e \to \infty\) for rare wins) are
   clipped to \([1, w_{\max}]\) with \(w_{\max} \approx 20\).
3. **Doubly Robust estimators**: combine IPW with a direct model to
   reduce variance. The estimator is consistent if *either* the
   propensity model or the outcome model is correct.

## Key Takeaways

1. In adtech, **calibration > discrimination**: a well-calibrated
   model with lower AUC beats a miscalibrated model with higher AUC.
2. The Brier Score decomposes into calibration (REL), resolution
   (RES), and uncertainty (UNC).
3. Log-loss is a strictly proper scoring rule. It incentivizes the
   model to report true probabilities.
4. Training data is selection-biased (bandit feedback). IPW corrects
   this by reweighting examples by \(1/e(x)\).
5. In production: Platt scaling or isotonic regression on a held-out
   set is the standard post-hoc calibration method.
